Chapter 01

What is AI, and how is it changing government data work?

"Artificial intelligence" sounds like science fiction. The AI tools you'll use in this handbook are much more ordinary than that. They are language assistants: programs that have read an enormous amount of text and learned to predict useful replies.

Think of it as a very well-read new colleague. They've read every statistics textbook and programming manual, they never get tired, and they answer instantly. But it's their first week. They don't know your department, they sometimes sound confident when they're wrong, and you would never let them sign off a report without checking it.

What changes for you

Until recently, analysing a spreadsheet of 50,000 service requests meant either learning to program or waiting in a queue for the analytics team. Now you can describe what you want in normal sentences, and the AI writes the instructions (the "code") for the computer to follow.

Before AI assistantsWith AI assistants
Learn programming for monthsDescribe the task in plain sentences
Search forums for error messagesPaste the error and ask "what went wrong?"
Wait for an analyst to explain a resultAsk "what does this mean for my department?"
Excel struggles past ~1 million rowsR and Python handle millions of rows easily

Common myths

MythReality
"AI understands my data."It only sees what you show it. It guesses about the rest.
"AI is always right."It can make mistakes and sound sure of itself. You check the work.
"AI will replace analysts."It removes the typing, not the judgement. Your knowledge of context matters more, not less.
"I need to be technical."You need to be clear. Clear instructions beat technical vocabulary.
Chapter 02

Meet your new assistants: R and Python

R and Python are programming languages. A programming language is just a strict way of writing instructions so a computer can follow them exactly, with no guessing.

A recipe for the computer. Excel is like cooking by hand: you click, drag and type each step. R and Python are like writing a recipe card. Once the recipe exists, the computer follows it perfectly every time, on any amount of data, and anyone can repeat it next month with new figures.
RPython
Born forStatistics and researchGeneral-purpose work, including data
Best atStatistical tests, survey analysis, publication-quality chartsCleaning messy files, automating routine tasks, text analysis
Who uses itStatistical offices, health agencies, universitiesDigital teams, data engineers, analysts
CostFreeFree
Pick it ifYour colleagues already use RYou're starting fresh with no preference

Both are excellent. For the tasks in this handbook, either one works. Use whichever your team already has, or whichever your IT department approves.

Why you don't need to memorise code any more

Your role shifts from writer to editor. The AI drafts the code; you:

  • describe clearly what you need,
  • run the code and look at the result,
  • ask the AI to explain anything unclear,
  • check the answer makes sense against what you know.

It's like using a calculator. You don't need to know how it does long division, but you do need to notice if it says 2 + 2 = 22.

Chapter 04

How AI writes the code for you

The instruction you type into an AI assistant is called a prompt. A good prompt is like a good work request to a colleague: it says what you have, what you want, and who it's for.

The five-step loop

  1. Describe your dataFile name, what each column means, roughly how many rows. Never paste real personal records.
  2. State your goal"I want to know which district has the longest waiting times." One clear question works best.
  3. Ask for code and an explanationName the language (R or Python) and ask for plain-English comments on every step.
  4. Run itPaste the code into your workspace and run it.
  5. Fix or refineIf you get an error, paste the exact error message back to the AI. If the result looks odd, say so and describe why.

The anatomy of a good prompt

PartWhat to includeExample
RoleWho the AI should act as"Act as a patient data analyst."
ContextWho you are, who it's for"I work in a city transport department."
DataColumns and their meaning"Columns: route, date, passengers."
TaskThe one thing you want"Find the 5 busiest routes."
FormatHow you want the answer"Python code with comments, then a summary table."

Chapter 05

Choosing the right analysis

The AI can run almost any statistical method. Its weak point is knowing which one fits your question, because it only knows what you tell it. If you name the type of question, you'll get a better answer.

A toolbox, not a magic wand. You wouldn't use a hammer on a screw. Each analysis is a tool built for one kind of question. Start by naming the question, and the right tool usually picks itself.

What kind of question are you asking?

Pick the one closest to yours.

All question types at a glance

QuestionChartUsual method
What's typical?Histogram or box plotMedian, percentiles
Do two groups differ?Side-by-side box plotst-test or Mann–Whitney
Do three or more groups differ?Sorted dot plotANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis
Do rates or percentages differ?Sorted bar of %Chi-square test
Do two measures move together?Scatter plotCorrelation
Is it going up or down?Line chartYear-on-year change, moving average
Which factors matter most?Coefficient plotRegression
What are people saying?Bar chart of themesTheme coding, word counts

You don't need to remember the method names. Put the plain-English question in your prompt and add: "Suggest the most suitable method and explain why in two sentences."

Chapter 06

Making sense of the numbers

Getting a result is half the job. The other half is knowing what it means, and what it doesn't. AI is very good at translating statistical output into plain language, if you ask well.

Why the average can mislead

Below are seven community grants. Drag the slider to make one grant much larger and watch what happens.

Average (mean)—
Middle value (median)—

The bus-stop test. If a billionaire joins ten people at a bus stop, the average wealth at the stop becomes enormous, yet nobody else got richer. The median, the person in the middle, barely moves. For incomes, grants, waiting times and costs, always ask for both.

Statistics in plain English

Average (mean)

Add everything up, divide by how many.

Easily pulled up or down by a few extreme values.

Median

The middle value when everything is lined up in order.

Half are above, half below. Robust to extremes.

Percentage change

How much something grew or shrank compared with where it started.

From 200 to 250 complaints is +25%, not +50.

Correlation

Two things tend to move together.

Ice-cream sales and drownings rise together. Summer causes both. Correlation is not cause.

Statistically significant

The difference is unlikely to be pure luck.

It does not mean "large" or "important". A tiny difference can be significant in big data.

p-value

Roughly: if there were really no difference, how surprising would this result be?

Below 0.05 is the usual "probably not chance" line. It's a convention, not a law.

Confidence interval

A range where the true value probably sits.

"Satisfaction 72% (68% to 76%)": the real figure is likely within that band.

Sample size

How many people or records the result is based on.

A district with 12 survey replies tells you far less than one with 1,200.

Asking AI to interpret results

Paste the output (a summary table, test result or description of a chart) and use this prompt. Output tables are usually fine to share, because they contain summaries, not people.

Template · "What does this mean for my department?"
I work in the housing department. Below is the output from my analysis of repair request waiting times by district.

[paste the output table or test result]

Please explain, for someone with no statistics background:
1. What are the 3 most important things this result shows?
2. What does it NOT show? What should I avoid claiming?
3. How confident can I be? Is the sample big enough?
4. What might this mean for my department's decisions?
5. What follow-up questions should I investigate before acting?

Use short sentences. Avoid jargon, or explain any technical term you must use.

Five questions for any chart

  1. Where does the vertical axis start?A bar chart that doesn't start at zero can make a 2% difference look huge.
  2. How many records are behind each bar?Ask the AI to add counts to the labels.
  3. Is it a total or a rate?Bigger districts will have more complaints. Per 1,000 residents is often fairer.
  4. What time period?A single unusual month (a flood, a strike) can distort a trend.
  5. What's missing?Who didn't respond to the survey? Which offices didn't report?
Chapter 07

Common AI mistakes in statistics, and how to catch them

AI-written code usually runs. That's the trap. Code can run perfectly and still give the wrong answer. These are the mistakes that come up most often in practice, with a quick way to spot each one.

High risk

Rows silently dropped

Many functions quietly skip rows with blanks. Your 10,000 records become 7,400 without warning.

Spot it: compare row counts before and after each step.

Prevent: "Print the row count after every step and tell me how blanks are handled."

High risk

Made-up numbers in the summary

The code output says 72%, but the AI's written summary says 75%. AI can "hallucinate" figures when it writes prose.

Spot it: every number in the text must appear in the actual output.

Prevent: "Only quote numbers that appear in the output I pasted."

High risk

Dates read the wrong way

05/07/2025 means 5 July in India and 7 May in the US. Code may guess the US format.

Spot it: check that months run 1–12 and no dates fall in the future.

Prevent: "Dates are in DD/MM/YYYY format."

High risk

Rows multiplied by a merge

Joining two files with repeated IDs can duplicate rows and inflate totals.

Spot it: totals suddenly larger than the published figure.

Prevent: "Check the join key is unique in both files before merging."

Medium risk

Wrong test for the data

Using a t-test on very skewed data, like waiting times, or on 1–5 ratings.

Spot it: ask "what does this test assume, and does my data meet that?"

Prevent: "Check the assumptions and use a non-parametric test if needed."

Medium risk

Misreading the p-value

"p = 0.03 means a 97% chance we're right" is wrong. So is "significant means big".

Spot it: the summary should report the size of the difference as well as the p-value.

Prevent: "Report the size of the difference with a confidence interval."

Medium risk

Totals instead of rates

The biggest district looks worst simply because it's biggest.

Spot it: ask "per what?" for every comparison.

Prevent: "Compare per lakh population (or per application)."

Medium risk

Cause claimed from correlation

"More staff training led to faster processing" when the data only shows they occur together.

Spot it: words like "caused", "led to", "because of" in the summary.

Prevent: "Describe associations only. Don't claim causes."

Medium risk

Averaging rating scales

"Average satisfaction 3.4" hides that 40% were very unhappy.

Spot it: a single average reported for a 1–5 scale.

Prevent: "Show the % for each answer and the % who agree."

Medium risk

Unclear percentages

"40% were rejected." 40% of all applications, or of those reviewed so far?

Spot it: a percentage with no stated base.

Prevent: "State the denominator for every percentage."

The self-check prompt

After any analysis, paste your code back with this prompt. AI is often good at reviewing its own work when asked directly.

Template · Review the analysis
Act as a careful senior statistician reviewing a junior colleague's work.

Here is my code and its output:
[paste code and output]

Check for these specific problems and report each as OK / Problem / Can't tell:
1. Rows lost or duplicated at any step

Chapter 08

Working with survey data

Much government analysis starts with a survey: citizen satisfaction, household conditions, staff feedback. Surveys follow a few special rules. If nobody tells the AI about them, it will treat the survey like an ordinary spreadsheet and get the answer subtly wrong.

Tasting the soup. You don't drink the whole pot to check the salt. One spoonful is enough, as long as you stir first. A survey is the spoonful; the population is the pot. Random sampling is the stirring. Everything in this chapter is about making sure the spoonful tastes like the pot.

1. Survey weights

Sometimes some groups end up over-represented in a survey. Weights correct for this. Each respondent counts for more or less than one person, depending on how many people like them were missed.

GroupRespondents% satisfiedShare of real population
Urban60070%40%
Rural40050%60%
Unweighted result62%Too many urban replies pull it up
Weighted result58%0.4 × 70% + 0.6 × 50%

If your file has a weight column (in NSS and PLFS data it's often called a multiplier), always tell the AI its name and ask it to use survey-aware methods. In R these are the survey and srvyr packages; in Python, samplics.

2. Rating scales (Likert questions)

"Strongly disagree" to "strongly agree" answers are categories, not measurements. The gap between "agree" and "strongly agree" isn't necessarily the same as between "neutral" and "agree".

  • Report the percentage for each answer, and the share who agree (agree + strongly agree).
  • Use a diverging stacked bar chart, with negative answers on the left and positive on the right.
  • An average score is fine as an extra, never on its own.

3. Margin of error

A survey result is an estimate. The margin of error tells you how far off it might be. Roughly, at 95% confidence for a simple random sample:

RespondentsMargin of errorPlain English
100± 9.8 points"60%" could really be 50% to 70%
400± 4.9 pointsFine for a broad picture
1,000± 3.1 pointsTypical for a state-level survey
2,500± 2.0 pointsGood enough to track small changes

Surveys that sample villages or blocks first (cluster sampling) have larger margins than this table shows. Ask the AI to calculate them using the survey design.

4. Small groups and privacy

  • Don't report results for very small groups. A common rule is to suppress any cell with fewer than 10 respondents; follow your organisation's own threshold.
  • Small groups are unreliable, and they can identify people. "The one female engineer in Block 4 was dissatisfied" is a privacy breach.
  • Combine small categories (e.g. age bands) or report "fewer than 10".

5. Who didn't answer?

If only satisfied (or only angry) citizens reply, the result is biased however large the sample. Always report the response rate and ask whether non-responders might differ.

Template · Analyse a survey properly
Act as a survey statistician helping a government analyst.

My survey file citizen_survey.csv has:
- weight: survey weight for each respondent
- district: 12 districts
- q5_satisfaction: 1 = very dissatisfied ... 5 = very satisfied
- age_group, gender

Write R code using survey-aware methods that:
1. Uses the weight column for all estimates
2. Shows the weighted % for each answer to q5, overall and by district
3. Reports the % satisfied (4 or 5) with a 95% confidence interval
4. Hides any district/group cell with fewer than 10 respondents
5. Draws a diverging stacked bar chart

Explain each step in plain English and say what the confidence intervals mean.
Chapter 09

Real-world government examples

Three realistic cases. In each one, the staff member never writes code. They describe, run, check and interpret. Open each scenario to follow along.

Case AUnderstanding 4,800 citizen feedback comments

Situation. Priya in a municipal services office has a year of online feedback: a satisfaction score from 1 to 5 and a free-text comment. Her director wants to know "what people are unhappy about".

Safety first. She removes names, phone numbers and email addresses before analysis, and runs the code in her department's approved desktop tool, not an online chatbot.

What she asked the AI. She described the columns (no real comments) and asked for Python code to: count responses by month, find the most common words and phrases in low-score comments, and group comments into themes.

Finding38% of low scores mention waiting or "no reply"
FindingComplaints doubled after the phone line changed in March
CheckShe read 30 random comments to confirm the themes were real

Lesson. AI found the patterns fast; Priya's reading of real comments confirmed they meant what the AI thought.

Case BIs the infrastructure budget fairly distributed?

Situation. Arun in a state finance office is asked whether road maintenance money reaches rural districts fairly. The budget file is already published on the open-data portal, so it is safe to use.

What he asked the AI. R code to calculate spending per kilometre of road and per resident for each district, rank them, and draw a sorted bar chart.

First resultUrban districts get 3× the total money
Better resultPer kilometre of road, rural districts get 40% less

Lesson. Totals and rates told different stories. Asking "per what?" changed the conclusion. Arun's briefing note says what the data can and cannot show.

Situation. Meera, a planning officer, hears that permits are slower this year. She has 3 years of permit records with the application date, decision date and permit type.

Step 6 · Interpretation

  • Totals mislead here. Nandpur received the most grievances (1,590) but has the lowest rate per lakh people (41.4) and resolves 83% on time.
  • Shivgarh needs attention. It has the highest rate per lakh (60.1), the lowest on-time resolution (57.2%), and a typical wait of about 33 days.
  • Monsoon pressure. Grievances rise by about 40% in July–August (598 and 604 against roughly 420 a month), and on-time resolution falls to about 71%.
  • No year-long decline. Outside the monsoon months, resolution stays in the 74–83% range.
  • What the data can't say. It doesn't explain why Shivgarh is slower. Staffing, the type of grievances or geography would need checking.

Chapter 11

Writing it up: briefing notes and AI disclosure

Analysis only matters once someone reads it and acts. Senior officers have little time, so the finding goes first, the method last, and every number must be checkable.

Example, built from Chapter 10

Briefing note: Grievance redressal performance, FY 2025–26

Purpose

To identify districts struggling with grievance redressal and check whether performance is worsening.

Key findings
  • Shivgarh resolves 57% of grievances within 30 days, against 73–84% in the other five districts.
  • Shivgarh also receives the most grievances per person: 60 per lakh people, against 41–56 elsewhere.
  • Grievances rise by about 40% in July–August each year, and on-time resolution falls to about 71%.
What it means

Shivgarh's problem is not volume alone; it is slower per case. The monsoon peak affects every district and is predictable.

Limitations
  • The data does not record grievance type or staffing, so the causes are not yet known.
Recommendation

Review staffing and case mix in Shivgarh, and plan temporary capacity for July–August from next year.

Method note

Monthly grievance records, Apr 2025 – Mar 2026, 6 districts. Analysis in Python (pandas, SciPy). Code drafted with an AI assistant and checked by the analyst; figures verified against source totals.

Template · Draft a briefing note
Act as an experienced government policy writer.

Using ONLY the results below, draft a one-page briefing note for the District Collector.
[paste your summary table and key outputs]

Structure: Purpose (1 sentence), Key findings (3 bullets, each with a number), What it means (2–3 sentences), Limitations (1–2 bullets), Recommendation (1–2 sentences), Method note.

Rules:
- Use only numbers that appear in my results. Do not calculate new ones.
- Plain English, short sentences, no jargon.
- Round sensibly (57%, not 57.21%). Use Indian number format (lakh, crore).
- Describe associations, not causes.

Plain-number rules

  • Round: "57%" not "57.21%"; "about ₹4.2 crore" not "₹4,21,63,880".
  • Use comparisons people can picture: "about 6 in 10" or "nearly twice as high".
  • Always give the base: "57% of the 589 grievances received".
  • Pick one number system and stick to it: lakh/crore for Indian audiences, million/billion for international ones.

Saying that AI helped

Being open about AI use builds trust and lets reviewers check the right things. Use a short line in routine notes and a fuller statement in published reports.

Disclosure · Short version
Analysis code was drafted with the help of an AI assistant (tool name) and reviewed, run and verified by [name/team]. No personal data was shared with the AI tool.
Disclosure · Full version
Use of AI in this report
- Tool: [AI tool and model], accessed via [department-approved platform / API / local installation]
- Used for: drafting analysis code in R/Python; first draft of plain-language explanations
- Not used for: final conclusions, recommendations or decisions about individuals
- Data shared with the tool: column descriptions and summary tables only; no personal or confidential records
- Verification: all figures were reproduced from source data by [name] and reviewed by [name] on [date]
- Code and prompts are retained at: [location]
Chapter 12

Safety and ethics

Public trust depends on handling citizens' information carefully. Most public AI chat tools send what you type to external servers. Treat the chat box like an email to someone outside your organisation.

Golden ruleNever paste or upload personal or confidential data into an AI tool your department hasn't approved for that data. Follow your organisation's AI and data-protection policy; it overrides anything in this handbook.

If you work in India

Law or ruleWhat it means for you
Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023India's main data-protection law. It covers digital personal data, including citizen records held by government bodies.
DPDP Rules, 2025Notified on 13 November 2025 and phased in. Most duties (notice, consent, security safeguards, breach reporting, retention) apply from 13 May 2027.
PenaltiesUp to ₹250 crore for failing to take reasonable security safeguards to protect personal data.
Government exemptionsSome processing by the State is exempt for specific purposes. Don't assume yours is: ask your department's data protection or legal officer.
AadhaarAadhaar numbers have their own restrictions under the Aadhaar Act. Never paste them into any AI tool.

In practice: pasting citizen records into an unapproved AI tool could amount to a personal data breach. The "share the shape, not the data" method below avoids the problem entirely.

The traffic-light test

Green · fine to share

  • Published open data
  • Column names and descriptions
  • Made-up example rows
  • Summary tables with no individuals
  • Error messages (check they don't contain data)

Amber · check first

  • Internal reports not yet published
  • Draft budget figures
  • Small-group summaries (e.g. fewer than 10 people)
  • Anything marked "internal"

Red · never share

  • Names, addresses, phone numbers, ID numbers
  • Health, welfare, tax or criminal records
  • Children's data
  • Raw files of citizen records
  • Passwords or system access details

You can get excellent code without the AI ever seeing a real record. Describe the columns and give 3 invented example rows. The AI writes code; you run it locally on the real file.

Fairness and bias

  • Data reflects the past. If some communities report problems less often, low complaint numbers don't mean fewer problems.
  • Averages hide groups. Ask the AI to break results down by area, age band or service type where relevant.
  • Decisions stay human. AI analysis informs decisions about people; it should not make them.
  • Be transparent. Keep your prompts and code with the report so others can repeat and check the work.

Good habits for API use at work

  • Send summaries, not records. Model outputs and summary tables are usually fine; raw citizen rows are not.
  • Check your agreement. Enterprise or government contracts often include stronger data-retention terms than personal accounts.
  • Use a local model for sensitive work, if your policy allows AI at all on that data. Ollama keeps everything on your machine.
  • Set a spending limit. APIs charge per amount of text (counted in "tokens"). Set a monthly cap in the provider's dashboard.
  • Keep the prompt with the code. Prompts saved in your script make the analysis repeatable and auditable.

Exercise 2Compare waiting times in two districts

Task. Is the typical wait in Shivgarh really longer than in Meenakshi Nagar? Use the 12 monthly median_days values for each. Which test should you ask for?

Answer. It's a two-group comparison with only 12 values each and possibly skewed data, so a Mann–Whitney test is the safe choice (Chapter 05).

Exercise 3Spot the mistake

Task. An AI summary reads: "Nandpur has the worst grievance problem, because it received the most complaints (1,590)." What's wrong?

Answer. It compares totals, not rates (Chapter 07). Nandpur is the largest district. Per lakh people it has the lowest grievance rate (41.4), and it resolves 82.7% on time. The district with the biggest problem is Shivgarh.

Exercise 4Weight a survey result by hand

Task. A staff survey gets 300 replies from head office (80% satisfied) and 200 from field offices (45% satisfied). Field staff are 75% of the workforce. What's the weighted % satisfied?

Answer. 0.25 × 80% + 0.75 × 45% = 20% + 33.75% = about 54%. The unweighted figure, (240 + 90) ÷ 500 = 66%, overstates satisfaction because head office replied more.

AI for Statistical Analysis · Cheat sheetMahesh Divakaran

The 5-step loop

  • Describe the data (columns, not records)
  • State one clear question
  • Ask for code + plain-English comments
  • Run it, compare row counts
  • Paste errors back; refine

Every prompt needs

  • Role: "Act as a patient analyst"
  • Context: department, audience
  • Data: file, columns, date format
  • Task: one specific job
  • Format: language, comments, output

Pick the method

  • Typical value → median
  • 2 groups → t-test / Mann–Whitney
  • 3+ groups → ANOVA / Kruskal–Wallis
  • Rates → chi-square
  • Together? → correlation
  • Over time → line chart

Read results

  • Mean vs median: both, always
  • Totals vs rates: "per what?"
  • Significant ≠ important
  • Correlation ≠ cause
  • Check n behind every figure

Catch AI mistakes

  • Rows dropped or doubled
  • Numbers in text not in output
  • DD/MM vs MM/DD dates
  • Wrong test for skewed data
  • % with no stated base

Surveys

  • Use the weight column
  • Likert: % per answer, not just mean
  • n = 400 → ±5 points
  • Suppress cells under 10
  • Report the response rate

Never share

  • Names, Aadhaar, phone, address
  • Health, welfare, tax records
  • Raw citizen files
  • API keys or passwords

Before you publish

  • Spot-check 3 figures by hand
  • Totals match the source
  • Colleague review
  • AI-use disclosure added
